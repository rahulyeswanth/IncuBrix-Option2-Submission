# One recording, one week of content

Most creators publish one long episode and then move on to the next one. That is the most expensive habit in content. The episode already contains a week of posts, and nobody has cut them out yet.

Start by listening for claims, not topics. A topic is just a label, like "productivity" or "editing". A claim is a sentence someone could disagree with. Claims make people stop scrolling because they create tension.

Scan the recording in five minute windows. An hour of audio gives you twelve windows, and each window usually holds one strong idea. You do not need to find the perfect moment. You only need to find the clearest one in each window.

A good clip answers one question. If a clip answers two questions, cut it in half. Viewers should know what they will learn within the first two seconds.

Open with the payoff, not the introduction. Nobody needs your name before they know why the clip matters. Say the surprising sentence first, then explain it.

Never start a clip with a greeting. Hello and welcome are the fastest way to lose a viewer. Start on the sentence that makes your guest lean forward.

Keep every short video under sixty seconds, and aim for thirty. Short does not mean shallow. It means you removed everything that was not the point.

Add captions to every video, because most people watch with the sound off. Word by word captions are easier to follow than a block of text. Check each caption against the audio before you publish, because a wrong word destroys trust quickly.

Write the first line of the caption last. The first line is the only part most people read. It should promise something specific, not describe the video.

Give each platform its own version instead of posting the same file everywhere. LinkedIn readers want a lesson at work. Instagram viewers want a visual and a quick idea. Shorts viewers want one fast answer. The idea can be the same while the packaging changes.

Turn your best three clips into carousels. A carousel lets someone save the idea and come back to it later. Saves are a signal that the content was useful, not just entertaining.

Use one idea per slide and at most twenty words. If a slide needs a paragraph, it is two slides. The last slide should tell people what to do next.

Do not post everything on day one. Spread one recording across five or six days so each post gets its own moment. The audience that missed Monday will still see Thursday.

Reuse the same idea in a different shape. A claim becomes a video on Monday, a quote card on Tuesday and a carousel on Wednesday. Repeating an idea is not lazy when each version teaches it differently.

Track one number per post, not ten. Pick watch time for videos and saves for carousels. When you only watch one number, you can actually act on it.

Write down which clips worked and why. After a month you will see a pattern in the hooks that earn attention. That pattern is more valuable than any single viral post.

Batch the work into one session each week. Cutting, captioning and scheduling all at once is faster than doing a little every day. Switching tasks is where the hours disappear.

Always keep the original recording. Every clip you cut is a copy, and the long episode stays your source of truth. If you ever need to fix a mistake, you can go back and cut it again.

Ask permission before you publish anyone else's voice or face. A guest agreed to the full conversation, not necessarily to every clip. A quick message protects the relationship and costs nothing.

Label anything made with AI where the platform asks you to. Honesty about your process builds more trust than hiding it. Audiences forgive tools. They rarely forgive being misled.

Finish every video with one clear next step. Tell people to save it, send it to a friend or watch the full episode. If you ask for three things, people do none of them.

The goal is not to post more. The goal is to make each recording travel further. One careful recording, cut well, can carry your message for a whole week.
